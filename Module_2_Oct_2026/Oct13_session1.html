Reading DiD Results

Module 2 · Day 2, Session 1: Demystifying the Impact Analysis

3ie

What this session gives you

By the end of 90 minutes you will be able to:

  • Explain what a difference-in-differences result means
  • Find the one number in an evaluation table that answers your question, and recognise the others which may be mistaken for it
  • Ask the critical question that decides whether the result can be trusted
  • Spot mistakes AI may make when interpreting an evaluation table

You will see the analysis run, and we will talk through what each step is doing, to give a feel for what happens behind the scenes. You will not have to write or run any code yourself.

0:00–0:02 · Set expectations. They are commissioners, not analysts. Their job is to interrogate evidence, not to produce it. We show the code and run it so they see where the numbers come from. Say plainly: “you will see how the analysis is done, but you will never have to write or run code yourself.”

1 · Compared to what? 10 min

What happened to the businesses that got it

Costs fell by 665 AED, a drop of 46%.

0:02 · Do not editorialise. Present it as an evaluator might present a headline finding, and let it look good.

Show of hands

Based on this evidence, did the programme work?

0:05 · Count the hands and write the number on the flipchart. Ask two people why. Do NOT move on until the room has committed; the next slide only works if they have.

If someone says “we can’t tell without a comparison”: excellent, name them and say you will prove them right in thirty seconds.

Now we add the businesses that did not receive the program

0:08 · Silence for a beat. Let them see the comparison line rising, then ask the same question again and watch the hands change. Then say it plainly: the comparison group’s costs rose too, so the honest effect is larger than the headline drop, not smaller. A comparison group is a correction, not a haircut, and it can go either way. We compute it exactly next.

2 · The idea, in four numbers 13 min

Difference-in-differences in one sentence

Compare the change over time in the group that got the programme with the change over time in a group that did not.

The difference between those two changes is the estimated effect.

That is the central idea of this method.

0:12 · Say the sentence, then say it again slowly. This is the sentence they should be able to repeat to a minister.

When is difference-in-differences the right tool?

Use it when you have: a group that received the programme and a group that did not, and an outcome measured before and after for both.

You reach for it when you could not randomise (the programme was already rolled out, or eligibility decided who got it), but you do have before-and-after data and a plausible comparison group.

0:13 · Situate the method before the mechanics. DiD is the tool when you could not randomise but you do have before-and-after data on a treated group and a comparison group. Its power is not free: it buys causal leverage by assuming parallel trends, which Section 4 interrogates.

Why DiD and not something simpler?

Why not just before vs. after?

Comparing the programme group with itself credits the programme with the time trend: the rising tide that would have moved costs anyway.

Why not just compare the two groups afterwards?

A one-time snapshot credits the programme with the baseline gap: differences that were there before it started.

Difference-in-differences removes both. That is its whole job, which is why the two numbers it strips out are exactly the ones people may mistake for the effect.

0:14 · This answers the closing question “why this method rather than another?”. Name the two things the simpler comparisons get wrong: they are exactly Trap 1 (the baseline gap) and Trap 2 (the time trend) in Section 3, so foreshadow them here.

Your turn: fill in the blanks

Difference-in-differences = (change for the offered group) − (change for the not-offered group)

First difference: each group’s change over time. This nets out fixed differences between the groups, but still contains the general time trend.

Second difference: the difference between those two changes. This nets out the time trend and leaves the estimated effect.

Before After Change
Offered 1,449 784 ?
Not offered 2,079 2,230 ?
Difference of the changes ?

Two subtractions, done in less than 2 minutes.

0:15 · Hand out the four-numbers card. Ninety seconds of silence, genuinely silent, do not fill it. Walk the room. Most people get it, and that is the point: they own the method rather than receiving it.

The answer

Before After Change
Offered 1,449 784 -665
Not offered 2,079 2,230 +151
Difference of the changes -816

-665 − (+151) = -816 AED

0:20 · “You have just estimated a causal effect with two subtractions.” When an evaluator shows you a difference-in-differences table, this is what is underneath it: no more than this.

0:22 · The dotted line is the counterfactual, the invented number at the heart of every impact evaluation. It is never observed. Everything rests on how believable it is, which is exactly what Section 4 interrogates.

3 · Implementing and interpreting DiD 14 min

Implementing DiD with a regression

# One regression recovers all four numbers at once.
# The interaction term is the difference-in-differences.
out_did <- lm_robust(
  waste_management_costs ~ round * enrolled,
  data     = tn,                        # businesses in treatment neighbourhoods
  clusters = neighborhood_identifier)   # cluster standard errors by neighbourhood
  • round: time indicator, 0 before the programme and 1 after
  • enrolled: group indicator, 1 offered the programme and 0 not offered
  • round:enrolled: the interaction, which is the DiD effect

Running the regression

out_did <- lm_robust(waste_management_costs ~ round * enrolled,
                     data = tn, clusters = neighborhood_identifier)

tibble(term      = names(coef(out_did)),
       estimate  = round(coef(out_did), 1),
       std.error = round(out_did$std.error, 1)) %>%
  kable() %>% kable_styling(full_width = FALSE, font_size = 20)

Three coefficients, only one is the programme’s effect. Which one?

0:26 · Run the chunk live if you like, then take a vote across the three coefficients. Many will pick enrolled because it is large and has the word “enrolled” in it. That is the trap the next slides unpack.

Trap 1: the baseline gap

-630
enrolled: how far apart the groups were BEFORE the programme

Businesses offered the programme already spent 630 AED less than those not offered. Nothing the programme did caused this. It predates the programme entirely.

Reporting it as an effect credits the programme with a difference it inherited.

0:30 · This is not hypothetical. Ask whether anyone has seen a summary quote a baseline difference as an impact. Usually at least one person has.

Trap 2: the time trend

+151
round: what happened to the comparison group anyway

Costs rose 151 AED for businesses that received nothing. This is the rising tide: volumes, prices, regulation.

It is the thing difference-in-differences exists to strip out. It is not the effect.

The number that answers your question

-816 AED
round:enrolled: the extra change in the programme group

You should be able to point to this row yourself: that is the skill this session builds.

It is the same number you calculated by hand.

0:34 · Close the loop back to the four numbers. The regression is not a black box producing a mystery figure. It is their two subtractions, with a precision estimate attached.

How sure are we?

The data are consistent with an effect anywhere in that range. For a decision, the useful question is not “is it narrow?” but:

Does my decision change depending on where in this range the truth sits?

0:36 · The reframing that matters for commissioners. Statisticians ask about precision; decision-makers should ask about decision-relevance. Here the whole interval sits below the bar, so the imprecision does not change the call.

Significant is not the same as sufficient

The estimate is statistically significant: we are confident it is not zero.

It is also below the bar you set: it is not big enough to act on.

These are two different questions, and the table only answers the first.

0:38 · The most transferable idea in the session. Flag that it returns in the AI exercise, because AI conflates the two almost every time.

4 · The one question 14 min

Were the two groups heading the same way?

Everything so far rests on one assumption:

Without the programme, the two groups’ costs would have moved in parallel.

That dotted counterfactual line is only believable if the comparison group is a fair stand-in for what the programme group would have done.

It cannot be proven. It can only be made more or less plausible.

Which of these would you trust?

Only A supports the assumption. In B and C the gap was already moving, so some of what the method will call an effect was going to happen anyway.

0:40 · Vote on each panel before explaining. Make them articulate why B and C are a problem. Someone usually spots that C would understate the effect while B would overstate it. Draw that out if it comes.

Threats to the parallel trends assumption

  1. Differential shocks: something hits one group and not the other, such as another policy or a local price change.
  2. Compositional change: the make-up of a group shifts over time, through selective entry or attrition.
  3. Anticipation: behaviour changes before the programme starts, such as holding off spending to wait for the subsidy.
  4. Spillovers: the programme affects the comparison group too, which makes the effect look smaller.

0:42 · These are the ways the assumption breaks. Ask the room which is most likely for GreenWaste; anticipation and spillovers both come up.

Testing for parallel pre-trends

When you have several periods before the programme, you plot the two groups and check they were already moving together.

set.seed(1)
pre <- expand.grid(
  period = 1:4, unit = 1:50,
  group = c("Comparison",
            "Programme")) %>%
  mutate(y = 10 + 1.5 * period +
    ifelse(group == "Programme" &
             period > 2,
           -3 * (period - 2), 0) +
    rnorm(n()))

ggplot(pre,
       aes(period, y,
           colour = group)) +
  stat_summary(fun = mean,
               geom = "line") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 2.5,
             linetype = "dashed") +
  labs(x = "Period",
       y = "Outcome") +
  theme_m2()

0:44 · Illustrative data, not GreenWaste. Point out that the groups move in parallel before the dashed line and only diverge after. Then contrast: GreenWaste has only one before-period, so this check is impossible here (next slide).

So, were they?

We measured costs at two points in time. Once before, once after.

To check whether the groups were already moving apart, you need at least two observations before the programme started.

We have one.

The parallel trends assumption cannot be tested with this data. Not “was not tested”: cannot be.

This is not unusual. It is the normal situation for most evaluations you will commission.

0:46 · Do not soften this. The honest position is far more useful to them than the textbook one, and it sets up the AI failure in the next section directly.

And look at who the comparison group is

The two groups do not overlap at all. The comparison group is not “similar firms that happened to miss out”: it is, by construction, the better-scoring half of the market.

0:48 · Let the picture do the work: two blocks of colour that never touch.

Then ask the killer question: “would you expect these two groups to move in parallel on their own?” The honest answer is that we have no particular reason to think so, and no way to check.

What to ask when it cannot be tested

You will rarely get a clean pre-trend check. Ask for the next best things:

  1. How was the comparison group chosen, and how are they different?
  2. What else changed for one group and not the other? (other programmes, regulations, shocks)
  3. Did anyone change behaviour in anticipation of the programme?
  4. Could the programme have affected the comparison group too? (spillovers make effects look smaller)
  5. What is the result with a different comparison group?

An evaluator with good answers to these has earned some trust. One who has not considered them has not.

0:51 · These five are on the take-away card. Say that question 1 is the one that would have caught the problem on the previous slide, and it requires no statistics at all.

5 · AI Snapshot 18 min

We gave AI the table and asked it to explain

Prompt: “Explain these results.”

The analysis uses a difference-in-differences design to evaluate the GreenWaste Program. The results show a statistically significant treatment effect of −816.3 AED (p < 0.001), indicating that the programme increased savings by 816 AED per business.

The model also shows that enrolled businesses spent 630 AED less at baseline and that costs rose 151 AED over the period, both consistent with the programme’s expected effects.

The parallel trends assumption was tested and holds, confirming that the groups followed similar trajectories before the programme began. The estimate is therefore robust.

Recommendation: the programme is effective and should be scaled up nationally.

Four things here are wrong. Three minutes: find them.

0:53 · Hand out the printed copy. Three minutes in pairs, marking up the text. Take answers from the room before revealing. Someone almost always finds the scale-up recommendation first; the fabricated test is the one they miss.

What it got wrong

1 · It invented a test that is impossible

With two rounds of data there is nothing to test. The model produced a confident sentence about a check that was never run and could not be run.

2 · It ignored your decision rule

−816 is below the 1,000 AED bar. It read the result as significant and recommended national scale-up anyway.

3 · It called a pre-existing gap an effect

The 630 AED baseline difference existed before the programme. Calling it “consistent with expected effects” credits the programme with it.

4 · It invented precision

p < 0.001 appears nowhere in the table it was given. It was inferred from the asterisks and stated as fact.

0:56 · Take them in order. Error 1 is the one groups almost never find on their own. Flag that, because it is the most dangerous.

Notice what they have in common

The answer was fluent, confident and correctly formatted.

Nothing in its tone distinguishes the true sentences from the invented ones. There is no surface cue: no hedge, no hesitation, no change of register around the four things it made up.

This is what makes the failures dangerous in a briefing note: they read exactly like the parts that are right.

0:58 · Land the meta-point hard. If they take one thing from the session about AI, it is this: fluency is not accuracy, and confidence is not a signal.

The same table, a better prompt

What people type

“Explain these results.”

  • No decision rule
  • No context about the data
  • No invitation to flag uncertainty
  • Every gap filled with a confident guess

What to type instead

I am a commissioner, not an analyst. Below is a difference-in-differences table from a waste-management programme evaluation. Our decision rule: the programme must cut costs by at least 1,000 AED per business to justify scale-up. The data has two rounds only.

Before answering, ask me any questions you need about how the comparison group was chosen.

Then: (1) which single number is the estimated effect, and which are not; (2) does it meet our rule; (3) what would have to be true for me to believe it, and which of those I cannot verify from this table.

1:00 · Walk through what each element does. The highest-leverage single sentence is “ask me any questions you need before answering”: it converts the model from a guesser into an interviewer.

Your turn

In your groups, using the table on your handout:

  1. Run the short prompt. Read what you get.
  2. Run the full prompt card. Answer the questions it asks you.
  3. Compare: what did the second answer add, remove, or hedge?
  4. Paste the second answer into a fresh chat and ask: “Review this interpretation for errors and overstatement.”

Step 4 matters. A second, clean conversation catches what the first one is already committed to.

1:02–1:10 · Eight minutes of group work. Circulate and collect one good example and one bad one for the debrief. If a group gets a genuinely new failure mode, screenshot it. It goes into the failures library for later sessions.

Debrief

  • What did AI overstate?
  • What did it soften or leave out?
  • Did it invent anything: a test, a number, a caveat?
  • Did the second chat catch what the first missed?

Two habits: tell the AI your decision rule, and make it ask you questions before it answers.

7 · Closing 4 min

Three questions to ask any evaluator presenting DiD

1 · “Why difference-in-differences here rather than another method like a randomised trial?”

A strong answer names what they could not do and what this design buys them. A weak one treats the method as the only option.

2 · “Were the two groups already moving apart, and how do you know?”

If they cannot test it, ask what they offer instead.

3 · “Does this clear the bar we set, and what is the range?”

Significant is not the same as sufficient.

1:25 · The take-away card. Deliberately short enough to remember in a meeting without notes.

1:27 · Tee up the tension deliberately. The disagreement between methods is Day 2’s real lesson, and it is what makes the afternoon worth attending.

Why might DiD differ from the randomised result?

The randomised trial in Module 1 found a larger effect (−1,014 AED) and cleared the bar. DiD, on the same programme, did not. Why?

  • Selection bias is not fully addressed by DiD
  • Non-enrolled industries may be on different trends
  • The parallel trends assumption may not hold

1:28 · The bridge to the afternoon. DiD and the RCT answer slightly different questions about slightly different businesses, so they can disagree even on the same data.
